### Introduction

Culture-based studies are limited by only a small proportion of taxa being culturable (Staley and Konopka 1985). This limitation matters in the phyllosphere, the aboveground surface of a plant, since there are fewer cells in the phyllosphere than other plant-associated microbiomes, such as the rhizosphere, which is associated with the plant roots (Koskella 2020). Understanding which bacteria are able to be cultured and which are missed is therefore important for interpreting studies that rely on isolates. A previous culture-independent experiment in our lab showed the presence of 638 genera in the *Salvia mellifera* phyllosphere. I was able to isolate 18 genera from the *S. mellifera* phyllosphere using culture media, and 12 of those were present in the culture-independent data, meaning that only around 2% of the genera detected were recovered in culture. This observation then raises the question of what predicts culturability in the *S. mellifera* phyllosphere. Specifically, I will test whether the relative abundance of a taxa predicts culturability, and whether cultured genera cluster on a phylogenetic tree. To do so, I will matching isolate sequences to community sequences, compare the abundance and phylogenetic placement of cultured genera, and visualize the results, while using bash and R.

### Acquiring Datasets
This project uses two datasets that both target the 16S rRNA gene, a marker gene used to classify bacteria. The first contains 89 isolates cultured from *S. mellifera* leaves on either King's Broth (KB), a nutrient-rich medium, or phyllosphere tea (PT), a nutrient-poor medium made by adding a whole-leaf *S. mellifera* tea to agar. The isolates were Sanger sequenced, and taxonomy was assigned with BLAST (Altschul et al. 1990) against the SILVA SSU Ref NR 99 database, version 138.2 (Quast et al. 2013). Because KB and PT isolates did not differ significantly in phylogenetic diversity, I will pool them. The second dataset is a culture-independent profile of 14 *S. mellifera* leaf samples, generated by Illumina MiSeq amplicon sequencing and containins about 4,400 amplicon sequence variants classified into 638 bacterial genera.

The datasets differ in two ways that I will address directly. First, they come from different sequencing platforms and cover a different but overlapping regions of the gene, so I will compare them only over the region they share. Second, the leaves were collected about ten months apart using the same protocol, so a genus absent from the community data may reflect seasonal change rather than rarity, and I will interpret unmatched isolates cautiously. The community raw reads are public in the NCBI SRA (SRX34736148; BioProject PRJNA1508980); the isolate sequences, generated for an undergraduate project, have not yet been deposited. Both datasets are stored on my laptop, and I will copy them into a working directory created with bash. The data will stay outside my GitHub repository, which will hold only the two proposals and the scripts for this project.

### Approach to Analysis

First, I will determine which genera in the community data was recovered via culturing. Using bash, I will use BLAST(Altschul et al. 1990) as I had done previously to assign taxonomy to my isolates, to compare the culture-independent community sequences against the 89 sequences from my isolate data using only the part of the 16S gene that both datasets share. Using awk in bash, I will filter the output to keep matches above a percent identity cutoff and minimum alignment length, and repeat the analysis at 95%, 97%, and 99% to show how the number of cultured genera changes as a check. I can then use R to label a genus as cultured if any community sequences matches an isolate, resulting in a table of each of the 638 genera being labeled as cultured or uncultured. Isolates that did not have a match to the community data will be reported separately and interpreted cautiously since the two datasets were collected about ten months apart. 

After the setup of the data, I can use this table to answer my research questions. In order to test whether relative abundance predicts culturability of a genus, I will use R to calculate each genus' relative abundance as a share of all sequence counts across the 14 samples. To compare the uncultured and cultured genera, I will use a Wilcox rank-sum test in R, which does not assume a normal distribution and suits a small cultured group, and fit a Firth logistic regression with the logistf package, which is designed for rare outcomes like my roughly 12 cultured genera. I will also calculate the the percentage of community sequences that the cultured genera account for. To test whether cultured genera cluster on the tree, I will select the most abundant sequence from each genus in R and extract it with seqkit in bash, then align these with MAFFT (Katoh and Standley 2013) and build a tree with FastTree (Price et al. 2010). In R, I will use the ape and picante packages to measure the mean pairwise phylogenetic distance among the cultured genera and compare it with 1,000 random sets of the same size drawn from all 638 genera. Cultured genera that are more closely related than nearly all random sets would indicate clustering.

#Visualization

I imagine the final project to contain approximately four or five figures to describe the results. These visualizations will mostly be done using R, but some Adobe Illustrator will be used to make the figures "prettier". To answer whether relative abundance predicts culturability, I will make a rank-abundance plot that has the cultured genera highlighted to show where they fall from most to the least abundant. I also will make a boxplot comparing the relative abundances of the cultured and uncultured genera on a log scale, since a few genera may be far more abundant than the rest. If abundance predicts culturability, then cultured genera should have a higher median in the boxplot. To answer whether the cultured strains are clustered phylogenetically, I will make a phylogenetic tree of all the genera with the cultured genera highlighted, showing whether they group together or scatter across the tree. A histogram of the mean pairwise distance from the 1,000 random sets, with the observed value for the cultured genera marked as a vertical line, will show how unusual that observed value is. Finally, I will plot the number of cultured genera at each identity cutoff (95%, 97%, and 99%) to show how sensitive the results are to that choice.

### Conclusion

In summary, I will be using bash and R to organize my data, compare isolate and community data, and use different statistical tests to see whether relative abundance and/or phylogeny are predictors of culturability for the *S. mellifera* phyllosphere. By matching isolate sequences to community sequences, I will test whether abundance and evolutionary relatedness distinguish the 12 cultured genera from the other 626, and I will present the results in figures that show where the cultured genera fall by abundance and across the phylogenetic tree. These analyses will help identify how much a culture-based study can miss in a phyllosphere study and help guide whether uncultured groups can use different culture approaches to study them.
