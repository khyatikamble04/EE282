Cultures of heterotrophic bacteria grown on media represent less than 1% of the microbes observed in an environmental sample, termed the “great plate count anomaly” (Staley & Konopka 1985). Reasons for this include the bacteria being nonviable, or being viable but unable to grow further on the media it exists in (Staley & Konopka 1985). In addition, bacteria in the phyllosphere, the aboveground surface of a plant, face harsh environmental conditions from water availability, temperature swings, desiccation, and UV exposure (Gaube et al. 2023). This results in lower microbial densities present in the phyllosphere, as compared to the rhizosphere microbiome, which is associated with plant roots (Koskella 2020). Culture-dependent studies are therefore biased towards bacteria that are easiest to grow, leaving taxa that are difficult to culture but important in the microbial community understudied, along with their role in community dynamics. Comparing cultured isolates with 16S sequences obtained through culture-independent methods allows for a way to measure this bias directly. Using bioinformatics tools to match isolate sequences to community sequences, I will test whether abundance and phylogeny predict which phyllosphere bacteria were successfully cultured.

Previously, I isolated 89 strains from the *Salvia mellifera* phyllosphere for to compare a standard, nutrient-rich medium (King's Broth, KB) and a custom whole-leaf "phyllosphere tea" medium (PT) in their effectiveness at isolating strains representative of the *S. mellifera* phyllosphere. The isolates were identified with Sanger sequencing of the 16S rRNA gene and BLAST was performed with the SILVA database to identify the taxonomy to the genus level for each isolate. Faith's PD and Unifrac (Lozupone et al. 2011; Lozupone and Knight 2005) showed no significant difference between the KB and PT isolates, so I will treat them as a single pool of culturable isolates. Additionally, using 16S rRNA sequencing, my lab has previously identified the bacterial community composition of *S. mellifera*, which was extracted the year prior. This dataset contains 4400 reads from 14 different samples of the *S. mellifera* phyllosphere, filtered to contain only bacterial sequences and contains relative abundance counts per sample. The 4400 sequences fall into 638 genera, 12 of which were present in the cultured isolates. Together, these datasets will allow me to compare the taxa detected in the community sequencing and their relative abundances, with the taxa I recovered in culture. By matching isolate sequences to community sequences, I can label each community taxon as cultured or not cultured and test what predicts that outcome.

With these datasets, I will address two questions. First, does relative abundance predict whether a taxon was cultured or not? Genera that were abundant on the *S. mellifera* leaves likely had a higher chance to be sampled and plated. Therefore, I predict that the 12 genera that were cultured were more abundant in the 16S community data than the other 626 genera. To test this question, I will compare the relative abundances of the cultured and uncultured genera and calculate the percentage of the community sequences that the cultured genera account for. If the cultured genera are not more abundant than the rest, then other factors, like nutrients provided by the media, may matter to culturability more than abundance does. For example, if the cultured genera span the full range from abundant to rare, or if there were many highly abundant genera that were not cultured, it could point to factors other than abundance.

 Second, does the culturability of strains cluster on the phylogenetic tree? Isolates that are more related to each other may share traits that allow them to grow in culture. Therefore, I predict that the 12 cultured genera are likely more related to each other than a random selection of 12 genera from the community would be. If these isolates are located at random on the phylogenetic tree, then culturability is not based on phylogeny and instead likely depends on traits that vary even among close relatives.

A general approach to answering these questions would involve preparing the data, comparing sequences, interpreting the data, and visualizing the data. I will first use bash to organize and process the isolate and community sequence data. Then, I will compare the isolate sequences to the community sequences, labeling each community sequence as culutured or not cultured. Labeling will allow me to address both questions outlined above to interpret whether culturability is based on abundance and phylogeny. For further analysis, R will be used, along with the production of figures.
